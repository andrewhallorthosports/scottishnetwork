Abstract

Aims
Hip and knee arthroplasty volumes are increasing among working-age patients with end-stage osteoarthritis (OA). This study aimed to assess pre- and postoperative work absence, return to work (RTW), workplace modifications, and the exploratory productivity impact associated with work disruption.
Methods
This retrospective cohort study included patients aged ≤ 65 years undergoing primary total hip (THA) or total knee arthroplasty (TKA) between April 2023 and March 2025 at a UK national elective orthopaedic centre (National Treatment Centre - Fife Orthopaedics, UK). Eligible patients completed outcome measures and a RTW questionnaire. Exploratory productivity modelling used gross value added (GVA)-based assumptions and national arthroplasty data to estimate potential productivity loss.
Results
Of the 92 patients included, 68 (73.9%) returned to work postoperatively. RTW was more frequent among patients aged < 50 years (n = 30/33, 90.9%) and after THA compared with TKA (n = 40 (85.1%) vs n = 28 (62.2%) respectively). Among those intending to RTW, 68/77 (88.3%) did so, with similar rates after THA (n = 40/44, 90.9%) and TKA (n = 28/33, 84.8%). Median time to RTW was 2.5 months (IQR 1.5 to 3.4). Initial work modifications were required by 46/68 (67.6%) patients, and nine of 77 (11.7%) patients intending to RTW either failed to return for joint-specific reasons, or lost/changed jobs due to their osteoarthritis or surgery. Preoperative work cessation occurred in 34/92 (37.0%) patients and was associated with failure to RTW (p = 0.004). Longer waiting times were associated with higher rates of preoperative work cessation, affecting 4.3%, 28.3%, and 33.7% of patients waiting three, 12, and 24 months, respectively. Illustrative GVA-based modelling estimated Scotland-wide productivity loss of £146 to 425 million annually.
Conclusion
Most working-age patients returned to work after arthroplasty, although many required work modifications. Preoperative work cessation was associated with non-return, and longer waiting list times were associated with greater modelled productivity loss. These exploratory findings support improved employment counselling, RTW pathway optimization, and prospective multicentre evaluation.

Results · Return to Work

Intention to return was the strongest factor associated with returning

Of the 92 patients, 77 (83.7%) intended to return to work, and 68 of those 77 (88.3%) did so. Intention to return was the strongest factor associated with return (χ2 = 46.302, p < 0.001). The crude difference between hip and knee (85.1% vs 62.2%) narrowed considerably once restricted to patients who intended to return (90.9% vs 84.8%). Most of the 15 patients who did not intend to return (n = 10, 66.7%) had coordinated surgery with retirement.

Return to work by age category differed significantly (p = 0.001), from 30/33 (90.9%) of patients aged under 50 years to 14/23 (60.9%) of those aged 60 to 65 years. In procedure-specific logistic regression, age was associated with return after TKA (p = 0.043) but not THA (p = 0.138). Sex, SIMD, BMI and ASA grade were not significantly associated with return for either procedure. Postoperative HOOS-12 and KOOS-12 scores were not associated with return (p = 0.242).

Results · Waiting Time & Preoperative Work Cessation

The longer the wait, the more patients stopped work before surgery

Over a third of the cohort (34/92, 37.0%) stopped work before surgery because of osteoarthritis, for a median of six months (IQR 1.5 to 18). These patients were significantly less likely to return afterwards (OR 0.233, p = 0.004). Waiting time itself was not associated with return to work (OR 1.02 per month, p = 0.643), but it was related to preoperative work cessation, with a steeper gradient after knee than hip arthroplasty.

Results · Exploratory Productivity Modelling

Modelled productivity loss rose with waiting time

Across the cohort, 22,498 working days were lost before and after surgery. Productivity was modelled using gross value added (GVA) per person, an approach already used to estimate the national cost of working-age ill health, and extrapolated nationally with Scottish Arthroplasty Project data. The authors are explicit that these figures are illustrative and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive health-economic estimates.

Only patients intending to return to work were included in the productivity analysis. Workers requiring modifications were treated as contributing full GVA, as no validated adjustment metric exists. National extrapolation of the waiting-time figures assumed all patients had equal waiting times. The difference in modelled loss from preoperative work cessation between waits of three and 12 months was almost £45 million per year across Scotland.

Strengths & Limitations

Reading the findings in context

Strengths: the study captures granular occupational detail that is rarely reported in younger arthroplasty patients, including intention to return, work modifications by job type, preoperative work cessation, and job loss or job change as an outcome distinct from simple non-return. Community Health Index linkage provided deprivation and waiting list data.
01
A single specialist national treatment centre with comparatively short waits
Streamlined, high-volume pathways may produce higher return-to-work rates and shorter absence than elsewhere, so the effects observed may be underestimates.
02
A novel, unvalidated questionnaire completed retrospectively
Recall bias is likely, particularly for dates and nuanced employment changes, and follow-up duration varied. Longer-term data are needed to identify patients who return initially but cannot sustain work.
03
Small sample size limited subgroup power
Analyses by procedure, age, BMI, ASA grade, job type and waiting time should be read as exploratory rather than definitive.
04
Productivity estimates rest on generalised GVA assumptions
The estimates are illustrative rather than comprehensive fiscal or health-economic costs. Individual income, tax, sick pay, benefits and household effects were not collected, and mental health, job satisfaction and employer flexibility were not assessed. Preoperative PROMs were not available for comparison.

Take-Home Messages

Most working-age patients returned to work after hip or knee arthroplasty, but a clinically important minority could not resume their previous employment, and two-thirds of those who returned needed work modifications at first.
Stopping work before surgery was associated with not returning afterwards, and longer waits were associated with more patients stopping work, particularly before knee arthroplasty.
Exploratory modelling suggests that work disruption from end-stage osteoarthritis carries a substantial productivity cost, which the authors suggest waiting-time targets should take into account alongside clinical outcomes.
The findings support earlier employment-focused counselling, proactive return-to-work planning, and prospective multicentre evaluation of occupational outcomes and waiting times.
